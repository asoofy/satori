summary: Choose the most precise word for the blank, using clues in the sentence.
---
## What it tests

A sentence has a blank, and you pick the word that is **most logical and precise** in that context.

## Strategy

1. **Cover the choices.** Read the sentence and find the clue: a colon, a "but", or an example that describes the missing word.
2. Predict your own word, even a simple one like "worried" or "lasting".
3. Pick the choice closest to your prediction, then reread the full sentence with it.

## Clue words

- **Contrast:** *although, but, yet, despite* flip the direction of meaning.
- **Support:** a colon, *because*, or a following example continues it.

## Common traps

- A word that fits the general topic but not the specific clue.
- A word with the right feeling but the wrong strength ("disliked" when the clue says "furious").

## Worked example

> Though praised for its camera, the phone drew ______ reviews for its battery, which died by afternoon.

"Though" sets up a contrast with praise, and the battery evidence is negative. Predict *negative*, then match: **critical**.

## What it tests

You identify the **main purpose** of a text, its **overall structure**, or the **function** of one sentence.

## Strategy

1. For purpose, ask *why the author wrote this*. Use verbs: argue, explain, describe, challenge, illustrate.
2. For structure, label each sentence's job in a few words: *claim / evidence / counterpoint / result*.
3. For a single sentence, look at what it does for the sentences **around** it.

## Common traps

- The right topic with the wrong verb (the text *explains*, but the choice says it *criticizes*).
- Describing content instead of function.
- A structure in the wrong order.

## Worked example

> Scientists believed X. New observations don’t fit X. So researchers now propose Y.

Structure: *earlier view → conflicting evidence → new explanation.*

summary: Predict how one author would respond to another.
---
## What it tests

Two short texts on the same topic. You decide how the author of one would respond to a claim in the other.

## Strategy

1. Summarize **Text 1’s claim** in one line.
2. Summarize **Text 2’s position** in one line.
3. Ask: does Text 2 **agree, disagree, or agree with a qualification**? Qualified agreement ("yes, but…") is the most common answer.

## Common traps

- Too extreme: "rejects entirely" when Text 2 only adds a limit.
- Mixing up which author holds which view.
- An answer about the topic that neither author actually says.

## Worked example

Text 1: "Dictionaries preserve endangered languages." Text 2: "Documentation matters, but languages revive only when children use them daily." Text 2’s author would call documentation **valuable but not enough**.
