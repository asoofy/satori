## What it tests

You read a short passage and pick the choice that best states its **main idea**, or a detail the passage actually says.

## Strategy

1. Read the whole passage once, without looking at the choices.
2. Say the point in **one sentence of your own**. Look for turn words ("but", "however", "in fact"). The author’s real point usually comes after them.
3. Match your sentence to a choice. The right answer covers the **whole** text.

## Common traps

- **Too narrow:** a true detail that is only part of the passage.
- **Too broad or extreme:** "always", "all", "the only" when the text is more careful.
- **Outside knowledge:** it sounds reasonable, but the passage never says it.

## Worked example

> Most people know sea turtles return to lay eggs on the beach where they hatched. Fewer know how they find it: researchers think young turtles "imprint" on the beach’s unique magnetic signature and follow it back decades later.

One-sentence summary: *Sea turtles may find their home beach by remembering its magnetic signature.* A choice saying "Sea turtles lay eggs on beaches" is true but too narrow, because it leaves out *how*, which is the point.

## What it tests

A claim or hypothesis is given. You choose the quotation, finding or piece of evidence that **supports** (or sometimes **weakens**) it.

## Strategy

1. Underline the claim and split it into its parts. A claim like "calm on the outside but anxious inside" has **two** parts.
2. Predict what proof would look like **before** reading the choices.
3. Keep only choices that address **every** part of the claim, directly.

## For experiments

If a hypothesis says "X causes Y", the best support usually compares a group **with** X to a group **without** X, and shows Y changing.

## Common traps

- On-topic but proves something else.
- Proves only half of a two-part claim.
- Reverses the claim, so it would weaken it, when the question asks for support.

## Worked example

Claim: *Remote teams produce fewer new ideas.* The strongest finding compares remote teams with in-person teams on **new ideas produced**. A finding about remote workers’ job satisfaction is on-topic, but it doesn’t prove this claim.

## What it tests

A table or graph comes with a claim. You pick the choice that uses the data **accurately** and **supports the claim**.

## Strategy

1. Read the table’s title, column headers and units first.
2. Figure out exactly what the claim needs: a comparison? a trend? a peak?
3. For each choice, check two things: **Is it true according to the table?** and **Does it match the claim?** The answer must pass both.

## Common traps

- **True but irrelevant:** accurate data that doesn’t address the claim.
- **Relevant but false:** misreads a number, a column or a year.
- **Half the story:** the claim compares two things, but the choice mentions only one.

## Worked example

Claim: "Print was still the most borrowed format each year, but digital formats were growing." A correct choice must show print leading **and** e-books and audiobooks rising. "E-book borrowing in 2023 was 21,000" is true, but one number can’t show growth.

## What it tests

The passage ends with a blank. You choose the conclusion that **follows logically** from what came before.

## Strategy

1. Find the evidence pieces. Usually there’s a rule, a situation and a result.
2. Ask: *"If all of this is true, what must also be true?"*
3. The right answer is often cautious ("may", "suggests"), and it never needs information from outside the passage.

## Common traps

- A leap: it goes further than the evidence allows.
- The opposite of what the reasoning implies.
- True in real life, but not supported by *this* text.

## Worked example

> Fur color changes with **day length**. Snow now arrives later, but day length is unchanged. As a result, ______

Day length hasn’t changed, so the fur still turns white on schedule, even when there’s no snow yet. The conclusion: hares may be white against brown ground.
