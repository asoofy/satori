## What it tests

You get bullet-point notes and a **goal** ("emphasize a difference", "introduce the novel to a new audience"). You pick the sentence that best meets that goal.

## Strategy

1. **Read the goal first**, before the notes.
2. Cross out choices that don't do what the goal asks, even if they're accurate.
3. Among the rest, pick the one that meets **every** part of the goal (e.g. both *a similarity* and *a difference*).

## Common traps

- An accurate fact from the notes that doesn't serve the goal.
- Half of a two-part goal.
- For "unfamiliar audience" goals: a choice that assumes the reader already knows the work or person.

## Worked example

Goal: *contrast the two planets’ orbital periods.* Only a choice that gives **both** periods and signals the contrast ("While Mercury… Neptune…") works.

## What it tests

A blank sits between two sentences or clauses. You choose the transition that correctly expresses their **relationship**.

## Strategy

1. Cover the choices. Read the sentence before the blank and the one after it.
2. Name the relationship:

| Relationship | Examples |
|---|---|
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, by contrast, still |
| Cause/effect | therefore, as a result, consequently |
| Example | for example, for instance |
| Addition | moreover, in addition, similarly |
| Restatement | in other words, that is |
| Sequence | next, finally, then |

3. Choose a transition from that category.

## Common traps

- "However" is the most overused wrong answer. Check whether there's really a contrast.
- "For example" needs a specific case of a general idea.

## Worked example

> Newton used a mirror instead of a lens. ______ his telescope produced sharper images.

Mirror → sharper images is cause and effect: **Consequently**.

## What it tests

Choices differ only in punctuation, and you choose the one that joins or separates the parts of the sentence correctly.

## Core rules

1. **Two independent clauses** (each could stand alone as a sentence) can be joined by:
   - a period, or a semicolon, or
   - a comma **plus** *and, but, or, so, for, nor, yet*.
   - **A comma alone is a comma splice, and it's always wrong.**
2. A **colon** follows a complete clause and introduces a list, an explanation or an example.
3. **Extra (nonessential) information** is set off by a matching pair: two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses.
4. Don't separate a subject from its verb, or a verb from its object, with a single comma.

## Strategy

Find the boundary where the blank is. Test what's on each side: *could it stand alone as a sentence?*

## Worked example

> The museum opened a new wing last ______ it features art from forty countries.

Both sides are independent clauses, so a **semicolon** works, and a comma alone doesn't.

## What it tests

Grammar inside the sentence: subject–verb agreement, pronoun agreement, verb tense, possessives, parallel lists and dangling modifiers.

## Checklist

- **Find the real subject.** Skip prepositional phrases: *The collection **of rare maps** includes…* ("collection" is singular).
- **Neither/nor, either/or:** the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject.
- **Tense:** match the time signals in the sentence ("last summer", "by the time").
- **Pronouns:** singular nouns (a company, an orchestra, *each*) take singular pronouns (*its*).
- **Modifiers:** an opening phrase like *"Walking through the garden,"* must be followed by **whoever** was walking.
- **Parallel lists:** every item has the same form.

## Worked example

> Having analyzed decades of records, ______

The next words must name the analyst: "**climatologist Ana Brito concluded…**", not "the conclusion was…".
